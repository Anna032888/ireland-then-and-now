Distances are small, weather is changeable, and the best day is usually the one with no plan in it.

Practicalities

Plan a trip

Ireland is small — you can cross it in four hours — which means the planning problem is not distance but choice. Here is what actually matters.

There is no such thing as bad weather, only the wrong clothes.

an Irish saying, usually said cheerfully

When to come

May, June and September are the best months: the light lasts until nearly eleven at night in June, the roads are manageable, and the weather is at its least hostile. July and August are warmest but busiest, and accommodation on the west coast should be booked months ahead.

Winter is genuinely quiet — some coastal places close entirely — but it is also when Ireland is at its most atmospheric: empty beaches, open fires, and the light on the mountains. Expect rain in any month. The Atlantic keeps the temperature mild: it rarely drops below freezing and rarely climbs above 20°C.

If you are coming for something specific, plan around it: the Galway Arts Festival in July, the All-Ireland finals in September, or the winter solstice at Newgrange, which is drawn by lottery each September.

Getting around

Outside Dublin and Belfast you need a car. Buses reach almost everywhere, but often once a day, and the places worth seeing are usually a few kilometres off the route. Driving is on the left, roads are narrow and the speed limit on country roads is higher than it looks.

The train network is comfortable but limited: it radiates from Dublin to Cork, Galway, Limerick, Waterford, Sligo and Belfast, with nothing crossing the west coast north to south. The Wild Atlantic Way, 2,500 kilometres from Donegal to Cork, is the road trip the country is built for.

In the cities, walk. Dublin, Galway, Cork and Kilkenny are all compact, and the centre of Galway can be crossed in fifteen minutes on foot.

Where to stay

A bed and breakfast is the classic Irish way to travel: a room in a family house, a cooked breakfast, and a host who will tell you which road to take. Standards vary, and the best ones book out early.

Self-catering cottages suit families and anyone staying more than three nights — the west and south-west are full of them. Hotels are concentrated in the cities and the bigger towns, and the country house hotel is a genuine Irish speciality: a Georgian or Victorian house with a park, run as a hotel.

Hostels are cheap, sociable and everywhere on the walking routes. If you are doing the Wicklow Way or the Kerry Way, book the whole chain of hostels in one go, because the beds are finite on a narrow route.

A realistic week

Days 1–2, Dublin: Trinity and the Book of Kells, Dublin Castle, a walk along the Liffey to the Custom House, an evening in Temple Bar, and a half-day at Kilmainham Gaol.

Day 3, Glendalough and the Wicklow Mountains: the monastic site in the morning, the Sally Gap and Lough Tay in the afternoon, then west to Galway. Days 4–5, Galway and Connemara: the city and the Claddagh, then Kylemore Abbey, the Inagh Valley and a night in a Gaeltacht village.

Days 6–7, Clare and Kerry: the Cliffs of Moher and the Burren on the way south, then Killarney, Muckross and the Ring of Kerry. If you have one more day, add Dingle and Slea Head — it is the best single drive in the country.

Pick two regions, not five. You will see more.
